This level uses [N1](/learn/convolutions/) and levels 6–7.

More layers should mean a smarter network. For years it didn’t: past a few dozen layers, networks got worse, not better.
Two small changes fixed it, and both sit inside every Transformer block you will build in level 16:

1. a **residual connection**, which adds a layer’s input back to its output;
2. **normalization**, which rescales numbers so they stay in a stable range.

## 1. Deeper should be better. Is it?

Backpropagation (level 6) sends the gradient down one layer at a time. Each layer multiplies it by its own slope.
If every slope is a bit below 1, the product of many of them is tiny.

Now a real stack. Each layer is `h ← tanh(h @ W)`, 16 numbers wide, with weights drawn from a normal distribution with std 0.8/√16.
That is a little smaller than the level-7 rule of 1/√16.

<Gate requires="depth-guess">

<DepthLab />

The lines marked “plain” are the plain stack. Going up, the signal (each layer’s outputs) shrinks layer after layer. Coming back down, the gradient does the same:
in [`demo.py`](/files/residuals-and-norms/demo.py), the gradient’s std is 1.02 at the top, 0.098 ten layers down, 0.0059 twenty layers down and 0.000135 at the bottom.
The first layers get almost no learning signal, so they barely change. A deeper plain network can finally be worse than a shallow one.

<Stuck q="Level 7 fixed this with a good starting scale. Why isn’t that enough?">

A good starting scale keeps the numbers in range on step one. Training then changes every weight, and tanh units slowly move into their flat ends,
where the slope is near 0. Thirty factors that are each a little different from 1 still multiply into something tiny or huge.
The fix has to keep working while the weights change. A residual connection does.

## 2. The residual path

<Gate requires="depth-guess">

A **residual** layer adds its input back:

$$
h_{\text{out}} = h + f(h)
$$

$f$ is the layer that learns, here `tanh(h @ W)`. Take one number to see what this does to the gradient. The slope of $x + f(x)$ is

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(x + f(x)\bigr) = 1 + f'(x)
$$

<Blank id="res-deriv" />

<Gate requires="res-deriv">

That 1 is the point. Even when $f$ has a slope near 0, the gradient still passes down at full size along the plain $x$ path,
the **residual path**. The layer $f$ is on the other side of the `+`: we call it the layer side. Ten plain layers with slope 0.1 multiply the gradient by $0.1^{10} = 10^{-10}$.
Ten residual layers with the same $f$ multiply it by $1.1^{10} \approx 2.59$.

Look at the lines marked “residual” in the lab: the gradient reaching layer 0 never shrinks to 0. In this stack it even grows, to about 11 times
its size at the top. The residual signal grows too, from 0.97 at the input to 2.33, 3.46 and 4.39 after 10, 20 and 30 layers in `demo.py`.
Each block adds something to $h$, so $h$ keeps getting larger. Numbers that keep growing are the next problem, so every residual network
also **normalizes**: sections 4 and 5.

<TryIt>

Set the weight scale to 0.5 and the depth to 50. How far down does the plain gradient fall, and where does the residual one stop?
Then try scale 1.5: what goes wrong for the plain stack now?

</TryIt>

<Deeper title="With many numbers per layer, why is it still “1 + something”?">

With 16 numbers per layer, the slope of a layer is a 16×16 table of slopes (one for each input and output pair), called the Jacobian.
For $h + f(h)$ it is $I + J_f$, where $I$ is the identity table: 1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere.
Going down $L$ blocks multiplies $L$ such tables. Expanded, the product contains $I \cdot I \cdots I = I$ as one of its terms,
so there is always a route on which the gradient isn’t shrunk at all.

</Deeper>

</Gate>

</Gate>

## 3. A residual block in a convolutional network

<Gate requires="res-deriv">

Residual networks for pictures stack many blocks like this one. The layer side has two 3×3 convolutions (level N1),
each followed by BatchNorm (section 4) and a ReLU. The residual path carries the block’s input around them, unchanged.

<ResBlockDiagram />

The `+` adds two tensors cell by cell, so $F(x)$ must have exactly the shape of $x$. A 3×3 convolution with padding 1 keeps the
height and width (level N1), and its number of filters sets the number of output channels.

<Blank id="resblock-shape" />

<Deeper title="What if a block changes the number of channels?">

Then $x$ and $F(x)$ no longer match. The residual path gets its own small layer, usually a 1×1 convolution: it mixes the channels at each pixel
into the new count without looking at neighbors. With stride 2 it also halves the height and width, to match a layer side that shrinks the picture.

</Deeper>

</Gate>

## 4. BatchNorm: one feature, across the batch

<Gate requires="resblock-shape">

Residuals keep the gradient from vanishing, but the numbers inside the network can still grow or shift as training changes the weights.
**Normalization** puts them back in a stable range: subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation (std = √variance, level 10).
Afterwards the numbers have mean 0 and std 1.

The question is **which numbers** share a mean. Here is a batch of 4 examples (rows) with 4 features each (columns):

|  | f0 | f1 | f2 | f3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ex 0 | 1 | 4 | 2 | 9 |
| ex 1 | 1 | 0 | 6 | 3 |
| ex 2 | 5 | 1 | 5 | 1 |
| ex 3 | 5 | 7 | 3 | 1 |

**BatchNorm** normalizes each feature (column) using that column’s mean and std over the batch.
To get a std by hand: subtract the mean, square each difference, average the squares, take the square root.

<Blank id="bn-x20" />

<Gate requires="bn-x20">

Real BatchNorm adds a tiny number ε to the variance before the square root, so it never divides by 0. Then it applies a learned
scale and shift per feature, so the network can undo the normalization if that helps.

During training, the mean and std come from the current batch. At test time there may be only one example, so BatchNorm keeps a
**running mean**: after each batch it keeps 0.9 of the old value and adds 0.1 of the new batch’s mean.

<Blank id="running" />

<Predict id="bn-one" />

In a convolutional network, like the residual block in section 3, each channel is one feature. Its mean is taken over the batch
and over every pixel of that channel.

</Gate>

</Gate>

## 5. LayerNorm: one example, across its features

<Gate requires="bn-one">

**LayerNorm** uses the other direction of the table: it normalizes each example (row) using that row’s own mean and std.
It never looks at the other examples in the batch.

<Blank id="ln-x21" />

<Gate requires="ln-x21">

<NormAxisLab />

<Blank id="bn-count" />

<Predict id="why-ln" />

Transformers use LayerNorm, not BatchNorm, for three reasons:

- **Sentences have different lengths.** Short sentences in a batch get extra padding tokens (PAD), and BatchNorm would mix them into every feature’s mean.
- **Generation runs one token at a time.** When a model writes, the “batch” is often one sequence, where batch statistics are useless.
- **The same token should get the same answer.** With LayerNorm, a token’s output depends only on that token, not on which other sentences share its batch.

<Stuck q="If LayerNorm only looks at one example, how can it help training at all?">

It still does the job that matters: whatever the layer below produced, the next layer receives numbers with mean 0 and std 1.
The next layer’s weights never have to adjust to inputs that keep getting bigger or shifting. In level 16 you will see it after (or, in today’s models, before)
every attention and feed-forward step of the Transformer block, right next to the residual `+`.

</Stuck>

</Gate>

</Gate>

## 6. Write it yourself

<Gate requires="why-ln">

In NumPy, `X.mean(axis=0)` averages **down** each column, giving one number per column. `X.mean(axis=1)` averages **across** each row,
giving one number per row. `keepdims=True` keeps the averaged axis with size 1, so a (4, 4) table gives a (1, 4) or (4, 1) result
that broadcasts back over the table (level 1). `X.var(...)` gives the variance the same way, and `np.sqrt` takes the square root.

```python
X = np.array([[1., 3], [5, 7]])
X.mean(axis=0, keepdims=True)   # [[3., 5.]]      one mean per column
X.var(axis=1, keepdims=True)    # [[1.], [1.]]    one variance per row
```
